Research

Engineering quantum light, one atomic layer at a time

We create and control quantum emitters and spins in 2D materials using atomic defects, strain, electric fields and magnetism, then integrate them into devices.

Direction 1 of 5

Quantum defects and spin ensembles in 2D materials

Single atomic defects in 2D crystals can act as single-photon sources and optically addressable spins. We study defects such as the negatively charged boron vacancy (VB−) in hexagonal boron nitride, from individual emitters to dense ensembles for quantum sensing, and combine experiment with ab initio theory to understand and design them.

arXiv:2610.03547 (2026)arXiv:2007.14399 (2020)Optica 6, 542 (2019)
Direction 2 of 5

Strain and symmetry engineering

Bending an atomically thin crystal changes how it emits light. We pattern nano-ridges and other nanostructures to apply controlled, anisotropic strain. This lets us tune emitter energies, funnel excitons, and break the crystal's symmetry so that a whole ensemble of defects emits with one shared polarization.

Direction 3 of 5

Deterministic defect engineering at room temperature

Most defect emission in 2D semiconductors only survives at cryogenic temperatures. We use focused electron-beam irradiation to write optically active defects exactly where we want them, and compare host materials (MoS2, MoSe2, WS2, WSe2) to find which give the brightest, most useful emission at room temperature. Looking ahead to real devices, we also study scalable ways to grow monolayers, such as molecular beam epitaxy.

Direction 4 of 5

Seeing magnetism with light

Antiferromagnets switch fast and pack densely, but with no net magnetization their state is almost impossible to detect. We lay an atomically thin semiconductor directly on the magnet, where defects in the 2D layer act as light-emitting sensors: the color and brightness of their emission reveal the magnet's alignment and local field. Our NSF CAREER project pushes this proximity approach toward magnetic sensing at the scale of a few angstroms, with applications from quantum technology to wearable sensors for brain imaging. We also use light to program magnetic order itself, for example switching chiral magnetic states in the antiferromagnet CrPS4 and using them to control valley polarization in a neighboring WSe2 layer.

Direction 5 of 5

Quantum light in heterostructures and photonic chips

Charge-tunable van der Waals stacks let us load single electrons and holes into quantum emitters and switch their light with a voltage. We also put 2D emitters onto silicon nitride photonic chips, waveguides and cavities, the building blocks of scalable quantum photonic circuits. To build these stacks reliably, we developed a lens-enhanced optical transfer method that brings precision and scalability to van der Waals assembly.

What we've found lately

Advanced Materials · 2026

Optically tunable chiral magnetic states and exchange engineering via charge-transfer excitation in a van der Waals antiferromagnet CrPS4

Shaikh, Watanabe, Taniguchi, Chakraborty

Light can switch the chiral magnetic states of the van der Waals antiferromagnet CrPS4. Exciting its charge-transfer transition gives an optical handle on the exchange interactions that set the magnetic order.

ACS Nano · 2025

Magnetic proximity coupling to defects in a two-dimensional semiconductor

Shaikh, Whalen, Ho, Ishraq, Maurtua, Watanabe, Taniguchi, Ren, Janotti, Xiao, Chakraborty

Placing an atomically thin semiconductor on top of an antiferromagnet turns its crystal defects into optical sensors of magnetism. As the defects feel the magnet's proximity, their energy levels shift and their emission changes color and intensity, revealing both the magnetic alignment and the local field strength in a single measurement. Preprint · arXiv 2026

Symmetry engineering enables deterministic ensemble polarization control in hexagonal boron nitride

Ishraq, Herrmann, Agnihotri, Hutchinson, dos Santos, Asif, Murray, Puthiya Veettil, Stockl, Shaikh, Maurtua, Watanabe, Taniguchi, Doty, Dreyer, Janotti, Wang, Chakraborty

Ensembles of boron-vacancy spin defects in hBN usually emit with random polarization. Lithographically defined nano-ridge arrays apply anisotropic tensile strain that breaks the in-plane symmetry, so the whole ensemble's emission dipole lines up with the ridge direction. The degree of polarization scales with the strain anisotropy and does not depend on crystal orientation. This opens a route to polarization-defined spin readout and programmable photonic integration.

In review · 2026

Host material selection for room-temperature defect-related emission in monolayer transition metal dichalcogenides

Maurtua, Stockl, Puthiya Veettil, Shaikh, Ishraq, Shrestha, Thapa, Holtzman, Hone, Zide, Chakraborty

We gave four monolayer TMDs the same electron-beam dose and compared the room-temperature localized emission (LXD) that appeared. The host matters a lot: WS2 becomes the brightest, MoS2 shows the largest exciton shift and broadening, and MoSe2 keeps a clean exciton alongside strong defect emission. E-beam writing matches thermal annealing optically, with the added advantage of placing emitters exactly where they are needed.

How we work

From crystal to cryostat

We exfoliate and transfer monolayers, stack them into heterostructures, and pattern them with electron-beam lithography and irradiation. Then we measure them with photoluminescence, Raman and polarization-resolved spectroscopy, from room temperature down to cryogenic temperatures.

We work closely with theorists and crystal growers, including collaborators at UD, Columbia University, the University of New Mexico, the Molecular Foundry at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and NIMS in Japan, to connect what we see in the lab with atomic-scale models.
